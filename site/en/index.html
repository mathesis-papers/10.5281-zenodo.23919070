MathOverflow 516405, revision 1: the second adjacency eigenvalue of Apollonian networks and the fixed-rank extreme spectrum

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

The manuscript asks how the strongest eigenvalues of a repeatedly subdivided triangular network grow, and whether its second-largest eigenvalue has a particular limiting size. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer yes and give a broader description: every eigenvalue at a fixed position from either end of the spectrum is asymptotically governed by the degree of a corresponding early vertex.

Start with a triangle and a central vertex joined to all three corners. At each stage, place a new vertex inside every inner triangular face and connect it to that face’s corners; keep existing edges and leave the outer face untouched. After the first update, the centre has six neighbours, each outer corner has five, and each of the three new vertices has three. This shows how age and boundary position affect connectivity. The ordinary adjacency matrix records an edge by one and otherwise zero; acting on vertex values, it sums neighbouring values. Its eigenvalues describe modes that this operation simply rescales. The question connects these global quantities to local neighbour counts, rather than asking about a degree-normalized matrix or a Laplacian.

Write m for the stage and order the eigenvalues from largest to smallest, counting repetitions. The authors prove that dividing the second eigenvalue by 2^(m/2) gives a limit of √2, answering Rad Sarar Anando’s MathOverflow question 516405, revision 1. More generally, the first four limits are √3, √2, √2, √2. For each birth stage j ≥ 1, a further group of 3ʲ limits equals √(3/2ʲ); thus positions five through seven tend to √(3/2). Reading upward from the smallest eigenvalue gives the negatives of the same sequence. Each position must remain fixed while m grows. Equivalently, the extreme eigenvalue divided by the square root of the degree at that same rank tends to one at the upper end and minus one at the lower end.

The proof separates vertices already present at a cutoff stage from all later vertices. Temporarily discard edges within either group, keeping only edges crossing between them. This two-group graph has eigenvalues paired with their negatives. Their squares come from a smaller matrix: its diagonal entries count late neighbours of an early vertex, while its off-diagonal entries count late neighbours shared by two early vertices. A vertex’s incident inner faces double each stage, so its late-neighbour count grows exponentially. But for an early pair sharing a face, exactly one child face still contains both after subdivision. Their shared-neighbour count therefore grows only linearly with the number of subsequent stages. After division by 2ᵐ, the smaller matrix approaches a diagonal matrix. Its entries are three for the centre, two for each outer corner, and 3/2ʲ for each vertex born at stage j, reflecting their inner-face counts. Taking square roots produces the limits and multiplicities.

Discarding edges requires control, not just intuition. The authors orient later edges toward older endpoints: each newly born vertex has only three older neighbours. A bound using incoming and outgoing edge counts controls how much the discarded adjacency blocks can change eigenvalues. They combine this with an exact formula for the smaller matrix and an explicit finite-stage error estimate. First letting m grow and then advancing the cutoff makes the normalized discarded part negligible. Choosing the cutoff approximately halfway through the stages gives an error bounded by a rank-dependent constant times 2^(−m/4), for each fixed rank and sufficiently large m. The opposite limiting signs arise from the two-group approximation, not because the original graphs, which contain triangles, are bipartite.

The authors distinguish this result from related literature. Alan Frieze and Charalampos E. Tsourakakis established an analogous square-root-of-degree pattern with high probability for random Apollonian networks, where only one face is chosen per insertion. That is not the simultaneous deterministic construction here. The manuscript also acknowledges earlier spectral work by Andrade and Miranda, and Cardoso, Andrade and Souza. Maurizio Serva’s exactly solvable model changes the outer-boundary treatment and uses degree-normalized hopping, so its spectrum does not supply these constants.

The manuscript presents written proofs of the fixed-rank limits, finite-stage bounds, convergence rate and degree comparison. It identifies a pinned Lean declaration as covering precisely the original second-largest-eigenvalue limit, not all these extensions. The authors also report finite computational checks through stage eight; these test formulas and numerical eigenpairs, rather than prove infinite limits. The significance is an explicit deterministic account of both spectral extremes in terms of early vertices and their generations. It does not determine ranks growing with the stage, the full empirical spectral distribution, or normalized-adjacency and Laplacian spectra; those questions remain beyond the results established here.
